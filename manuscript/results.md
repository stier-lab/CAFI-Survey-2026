# Results

## Q1: CAFI abundance and richness scale sublinearly with coral size

Total CAFI abundance scaled sublinearly with colony volume (negative binomial GLM: β = 0.52, 95% bootstrap CI [0.44, 0.62]; z vs. β = 1: z = −11.45, p < 0.001, bootstrap p < 0.001; Fig. 2A), rejecting the Field of Dreams hypothesis (β = 1) in favor of Propagule Redirection (β < 1). Species richness also scaled sublinearly (Poisson GLM: z = 0.34 [0.27, 0.42]; z vs. 1 = −18.76, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). However, rarefied richness (expected species at n = 20 individuals) showed no relationship with volume (slope = 0.14, p = 0.64; dashed curve in Fig. 2B), indicating that the raw species–area relationship is driven entirely by passive sampling: larger corals accumulate more individuals and therefore more species, but do not support higher diversity per capita.

Per-capita CAFI density declined with colony size (log–log slope = −0.48; Fig. 2C), confirming density dilution — larger corals harbor fewer CAFI per unit volume, consistent with a finite propagule pool distributed across available habitat.

At the species level, 11 of 21 prevalent species (52%) showed sublinear scaling (Redirection; bootstrap CI excluding 1.0), 10 showed scaling consistent with Field of Dreams (CI spanning 1.0), and none showed super-linear scaling (Fig. 3A; Table S1). The inverse-variance–weighted meta-analytic mean β across species was 0.51 [0.46, 0.56], significantly below 1.0 (z = −18.8, p < 0.001). Obligate symbionts — Trapezia crabs (β = 0.43) and snapping shrimp *Alpheus lottini* (β = 0.37) — consistently showed sublinear scaling, while facultative associates such as *Caracanthus maculatus* (β = 1.18) and the coralliophiline snail *Galeropsis monodonta* (β = 1.27) scaled proportionally.

Among the six taxonomic groups, five showed significant sublinear scaling: Trapezia crabs (β = 0.43), shrimps (β = 0.50), other crabs (β = 0.47), fish (β = 0.74), and other invertebrates (β = 0.50). Gastropods were the sole exception (β = 0.94, p vs. 1 = 0.60), scaling proportionally (Fig. 3B; Fig. S9). All results were robust to taxonomic resolution: abundance β varied by <0.01 across five sensitivity scenarios, and richness z remained significantly sublinear in all cases (Fig. S8).

Species occurrence probability was size-dependent for 14 of 24 prevalent species (logistic GLM, FDR < 0.05; Fig. S15), with most showing increasing occurrence probability with coral size. This confirms that the sublinear scaling of total abundance reflects a pervasive pattern across the community rather than being driven by a few dominant taxa.

## Q2: Site pools and coral size structure community composition

Community composition differed significantly among the three reef sites (marginal PERMANOVA on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of Hellinger-transformed abundances: site R² = 0.06, p = 0.001; volume R² = 0.08, p = 0.001; Fig. 4A). All pairwise site comparisons were significant after Bonferroni correction (all p_adj = 0.003). Multivariate dispersion did not differ among sites (PERMDISP: F = 0.89, p = 0.42), confirming that the PERMANOVA result reflects true compositional differences rather than dispersion heterogeneity (Anderson et al. 2006). The site effect was robust across all five distance metrics tested (Fig. S2) and across 500 balanced subsampling iterations (100% of iterations significant at p < 0.05).

The three sites exhibited distinct taxonomic signatures (Fig. 4B). Maatea was characterized by high hermit crab abundance (33% of all CAFI, primarily *Calcinus latens*). Maharepa was dominated by obligate coral symbionts, with shrimps and crabs together comprising 71% of the assemblage. Hauru supported the most taxonomically balanced community and the highest proportion of coral-dwelling fishes (12%). Seven species drove the largest compositional differences among sites (envfit R² > 0.10, p < 0.01), including *Paragobiodon modestus* (R² = 0.46), *Fennera* spp. (R² = 0.39), *Calcinus latens* (R² = 0.39), and *Alpheus lottini* (R² = 0.27).

Community composition also shifted continuously along the coral size gradient. Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA), after partialing out site effects, revealed that coral volume explained 7.8% of compositional variation (F = 9.74, p = 0.001). This size–composition gradient was robust to rarefaction (2.6%, p = 0.001), confirming genuine compositional turnover rather than an abundance artifact. Species loading most strongly toward larger corals included *Trapezia punctimanus* (score = 2.95) and *Luniella pugil* (1.71), both obligate symbionts, while several gastropods were associated with smaller corals. Variance partitioning attributed 4.8% of variation to volume alone, 3.9% to volume–site overlap, and 91.5% to residual variation.

Community nestedness along the size gradient was not significant (NODF = 18.4, z = −1.09, p = 0.28), indicating that small-coral faunas are not subsets of large-coral faunas. Combined with the significant db-RDA, this suggests that species turnover — not passive accumulation — drives compositional change along the size gradient: some species are gained while others are lost as coral size increases.

CAFI abundance showed no significant spatial autocorrelation (Moran's I = −0.004, z = 0.24, p = 0.51), nor did richness (I = 0.024, p = 0.28) or Shannon diversity (I = −0.039, p = 0.77; Fig. S4), indicating that spatial non-independence does not confound our analyses.

## Q3: CAFI species richness predicts coral condition

Under a priori BEF hypothesis testing, species richness significantly predicted coral physiological condition (OLS β = 0.061, p = 0.018, Hochberg p = 0.036, k = 2; Fig. 5A). Total CAFI abundance was also a positive predictor (β = 0.19, p = 0.048, Hochberg p = 0.048; Fig. 5B). However, richness and abundance were strongly correlated (r = 0.77), complicating the attribution of variance to diversity per se versus abundance.

Variance partitioning clarified this ambiguity: of the total variance explained by richness and abundance together (beyond volume and site), 29.1% was uniquely attributable to richness, less than 1% was uniquely attributable to abundance, and 70.8% was shared (confounded) between them (Fig. S12A). A path model confirmed the asymmetry: the standardized path from richness to condition (β = 0.55, p = 0.20) was far stronger than the path from abundance to condition (β = 0.02; Fig. S12C). Together, these results indicate that the richness signal is not merely a by-product of abundance, consistent with the BEF complementarity hypothesis.

Rarefied richness (expected species at n = 20 individuals) showed no relationship with condition (p = 0.50; Fig. S13B), but this test is inherently ambiguous: rarefaction may remove either an abundance artifact or the BEF mechanism itself, since the diversity-mediated abundance pathway (more species → fuller habitat use → more CAFI → better condition) is the core prediction of complementarity theory. The variance partitioning and path model provide more informative evidence for separating diversity from abundance effects.

No exploratory predictor — Shannon diversity, *Trapezia* abundance, resident fish abundance, or *Galeropsis monodonta* abundance — survived BH-FDR correction (all p_FDR > 0.80; Fig. S13C), though effect directions mostly matched expectations (Trapezia positive, fish positive), except *Galeropsis monodonta*, which showed a positive direction despite the predicted negative effect. Community composition (PC1_CAFI) did not predict condition when tested separately in the supplement.

The reverse direction (condition → CAFI) was non-significant after FDR correction. Key species from the companion experimental study (8 of 11 with sufficient survey occurrences) were tested individually using Hochberg FWER correction; no species survived correction (Table S4). The survey had 85% power to detect the experiment's observed effect size (R² ≈ 0.12), indicating that null results for individual species are credible. Species-level analysis of individual condition traits (protein, carbohydrate, zooxanthellae, AFDW) revealed no significant associations after FDR correction across 95 species × trait tests (Fig. S16), consistent with a complementarity mechanism in which condition benefits arise from aggregate diversity rather than individual species identity. The nine strongest individual associations (Fig. S17) showed effect directions consistent with mutualism hypotheses (positive for guard crabs and shrimps). Landscape factors — coral volume, neighborhood density, and site — likewise did not predict condition (all p > 0.30).

## Supporting results (Supplement)

### Neighborhood effects

No neighborhood variable significantly predicted CAFI abundance (n_neighbors: β = −0.005, z = −0.89, p = 0.37; Table S5) or species richness (β = −0.003, z = −0.88, p = 0.38) after controlling for coral volume and site. Mean neighbor distance showed a significant negative association with richness (β = −0.005, z = −3.20, p = 0.001) and Shannon diversity (β = −0.007, t = −3.49, p = 0.001), but not abundance. No size × neighborhood interactions were significant (all p > 0.10). The power analysis (n = 61) indicated 65% power for medium effects (R² = 0.10), so null results should be interpreted as "no evidence of an effect" rather than evidence of absence. Compositional variability differed among neighborhood density categories (PERMDISP: F = 29.9, p = 0.001), though the linear trend in distance-to-centroid was not significant (β = 5.3 × 10⁻⁴, p = 0.53).

### Co-occurrence patterns

Volume-weighted null models revealed no significant pairwise co-occurrence after FDR correction (0 of 528 pairs significant at FDR < 0.05; Fig. S11; Table S6), though two pairs approached significance: *Harpiliopsis beaupresii*–*H. spinigera* (SES = −3.42, p_FDR = 0.053) and *H. beaupresii*–*Paragobiodon modestus* (SES = −3.47, p_FDR = 0.053). Intraspecific density analysis identified six species with significant mating-pair aggregation (FDR < 0.05): *Synalpheus charon* (SES = 10.5), *Alpheus lottini* (SES = 10.1), *Harpiliopsis beaupresii* (SES = 5.4), *Trapezia serenei* (SES = 4.4), *Alpheus pachychirus* (SES = 4.0), and *Trapezia bidentata* (SES = 3.7; Table S7).

### Cross-study scaling concordance

Seven species had scaling data in both the survey and companion experiment. Of these, two showed sublinear scaling in the survey but proportional scaling during experimental colonization (*Calcinus latens*, *Alpheus lottini*), one was sublinear in both studies (*Trapezia serenei*), and four scaled proportionally in both (*Caracanthus maculatus*, *Harpiliopsis spinigera*, *Galeropsis monodonta*, *Periclimenes watamuae*; Table S8). The contrast for *C. latens* and *A. lottini* — proportional scaling during experimental colonization but sublinear in established communities — is consistent with the hypothesis that density-dependent processes shift scaling toward Propagule Redirection over time.
