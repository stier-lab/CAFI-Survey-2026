# Results

## Q1: CAFI abundance and richness scale sublinearly with coral size

Total CAFI abundance scaled sublinearly with colony volume, consistent with Propagule Redirection (Fig. 2A). The negative binomial GLM estimated a scaling exponent of β = 0.52 (95% bootstrap CI [0.44, 0.62]; Wald z vs. β = 1: z = −11.45, p < 0.001; bootstrap p < 0.001), decisively rejecting the Field of Dreams hypothesis (β = 1). Species richness also scaled sublinearly (Poisson GLM: z = 0.34 [0.27, 0.42]; z vs. 1 = −18.76, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). However, rarefied richness (expected species at n = 20 individuals) showed no relationship with volume (slope = 0.14, p = 0.64; dashed curve in Fig. 2B), indicating that the raw species–area relationship is largely explained by passive sampling: larger corals accumulate more individuals and therefore more species, but do not support higher diversity per capita.

Per-capita CAFI density declined with colony size (log–log slope = −0.48; Fig. 2C). Because density equals abundance divided by volume, this slope is the algebraic complement of the abundance exponent (0.52 − 1 = −0.48), illustrating that sublinear scaling necessarily produces density dilution — larger corals harbor fewer CAFI per unit volume.

At the species level, 11 of 21 prevalent species (52%) showed sublinear scaling (Redirection; bootstrap CI excluding 1.0), 10 showed scaling consistent with Field of Dreams (CI spanning 1.0), and none showed super-linear scaling (Fig. 3A; Table S1). The inverse-variance–weighted mean β across 21 species was 0.51 [0.46, 0.56], significantly below 1.0 (z = −18.8, p < 0.001; Fig. S4). Obligate symbionts — Trapezia crabs (β = 0.43) and snapping shrimp *Alpheus lottini* (β = 0.37) — consistently showed sublinear scaling, while facultative associates such as *Caracanthus maculatus* (β = 1.18, CI overlapping 1.0) and the coralliophiline snail *Galeropsis monodonta* (β = 1.27, CI overlapping 1.0) scaled proportionally.

Among the six taxonomic groups, five showed significant sublinear scaling: Trapezia crabs (β = 0.43), shrimps (β = 0.50), other crabs (β = 0.47), fish (β = 0.74), and other invertebrates (β = 0.50). Gastropods were the sole exception (β = 0.94, p vs. 1 = 0.60), scaling proportionally (Fig. 3B; Fig. S7). All results were robust to taxonomic resolution: abundance β varied by <0.01 across five sensitivity scenarios, and richness z remained significantly sublinear in all cases (Fig. S6).

Species occurrence probability was size-dependent for 14 of 24 prevalent species (logistic GLM, FDR < 0.05; Fig. S12), with most showing increasing occurrence probability with coral size. Size-dependent scaling is thus common across the community, though 10 species showed no significant size dependence.

## Q2: Site pools and coral size structure community composition

Species accumulation curves confirmed adequate sampling coverage across all three sites (Fig. S1). NMDS ordination (2 dimensions, stress = 0.157) revealed site-level clustering along the first axis (Fig. 4A). Community composition differed significantly among the three reef sites (marginal PERMANOVA on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of Hellinger-transformed abundances: site R² = 0.06, p = 0.001; volume R² = 0.08, p = 0.001). Together, site and volume explained ~14% of compositional variation, with 86% residual — consistent with high stochasticity in community assembly. All pairwise site comparisons were significant after Bonferroni correction (all p_adj ≤ 0.003). Multivariate dispersion did not differ among sites (PERMDISP: F = 0.89, p = 0.42), confirming that the PERMANOVA result reflects true compositional differences rather than dispersion heterogeneity (Anderson et al. 2006). The site effect was robust across all five distance metrics tested (Fig. S2) and across 500 balanced subsampling iterations (100% of iterations significant at p < 0.05).

The three sites exhibited distinct taxonomic signatures (Fig. 4B). Maatea was characterized by high hermit crab abundance (33% of all CAFI, primarily *Calcinus latens*). Maharepa was dominated by obligate coral symbionts, with shrimps and crabs together comprising 71% of the assemblage. Hauru supported the most taxonomically balanced community and the highest proportion of coral-dwelling fishes (12%). Seven species drove the largest compositional differences among sites (envfit R² > 0.10, p < 0.01), including *Paragobiodon modestus* (R² = 0.46), *Fennera* spp. (R² = 0.39), *Calcinus latens* (R² = 0.39), and *Alpheus lottini* (R² = 0.27).

Community composition also shifted continuously along the coral size gradient. Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA), after partialing out site effects, revealed that coral volume explained 7.8% of compositional variation (F = 9.74, p = 0.001). This size–composition gradient was robust to rarefaction (2.6%, p = 0.001), confirming genuine compositional turnover rather than an abundance artifact. Species most strongly associated with larger corals in the db-RDA included *Trapezia punctimanus* (score = 2.95) and *Luniella pugil* (1.71), both obligate symbionts, while several gastropods were associated with smaller corals. Variance partitioning (adjusted R²) attributed 4.8% to volume alone, 3.9% to site alone, with negligible shared variation (<0.2%) and 91.5% residual. Compositional divergence among size classes was not significant after controlling for abundance through rarefaction (betadisper: p = 0.61; Fig. S3).

Community nestedness along the size gradient was not significant (NODF = 18.4, z = −1.09, p = 0.28), indicating that small-coral faunas are not subsets of large-coral faunas. Combined with the significant db-RDA, this suggests that species turnover — not passive accumulation — drives compositional change along the size gradient: some species are gained while others are lost as coral size increases.

CAFI abundance showed no significant spatial autocorrelation (Moran's I = −0.004, z = 0.24, p = 0.51), nor did richness (I = 0.024, p = 0.28) or Shannon diversity (I = −0.039, p = 0.77; Table S10), indicating that spatial non-independence does not confound our analyses.

## Q3: CAFI species richness predicts coral condition

Under a priori BEF hypothesis testing (n = 84 colonies with physiological data), species richness significantly predicted coral physiological condition (unstandardized OLS β = 0.061, standardized β = 0.27, p = 0.018, Hochberg p = 0.036, k = 2; Fig. 5A; Fig. S11A). Total CAFI abundance was a marginally significant positive predictor (β = 0.19, p = 0.048, Hochberg p = 0.048; Fig. 5B). However, richness and abundance were strongly correlated (r = 0.77), complicating the attribution of variance to diversity per se versus abundance.

Variance partitioning clarified this ambiguity. The richness model (richness + volume + site) had adjusted R² = 0.04, indicating modest overall explanatory power. CAFI predictors explained an incremental R² = 0.07 beyond volume and site. Of this increment, 29.1% was uniquely attributable to richness (~2% of total variance), less than 1% uniquely to abundance, and 70.8% was shared (confounded) between them (Fig. S10A). In the path model, the standardized coefficient from richness to condition (β = 0.55) was larger in magnitude than from abundance to condition (β = 0.02), though neither path reached significance individually (p = 0.20 and p > 0.50, respectively; Fig. S10C). The variance partitioning and the directional asymmetry in the path model together suggest that the richness signal is not merely a by-product of abundance.

Rarefied richness (expected species at n = 20 individuals) showed no relationship with condition (p = 0.50; Fig. S8; Fig. S11B). We address the interpretive ambiguity of this test in the Discussion.

No exploratory predictor — Shannon diversity, *Trapezia* abundance, resident fish abundance, or *Galeropsis monodonta* abundance — survived BH-FDR correction (all p_FDR > 0.80; Fig. S11C), though effect directions mostly matched expectations (Trapezia positive, fish positive). The exception was *Galeropsis monodonta*, which showed an unexpected positive association with condition despite being a known corallivore (Fig. S11D–E). Community composition (PC1_CAFI) did not predict condition (p > 0.10; tested separately in the supplement).

The reverse direction (condition → CAFI) was non-significant after FDR correction (Fig. S11F). Key species from the companion experimental study (8 of 10 with sufficient survey occurrences) were tested individually using Hochberg FWER correction; no species survived correction (Table S4). The survey had 85% power to detect the experiment's observed effect size (R² ≈ 0.12), indicating that null results for individual species are credible rather than underpowered. Species-level analysis of individual condition traits (protein, carbohydrate, zooxanthellae, AFDW) revealed no significant associations after FDR correction across 95 species × trait tests (Fig. S13), suggesting that no single species drives the richness–condition relationship. The nine strongest individual associations (Fig. S14) showed effect directions consistent with mutualism hypotheses (positive for guard crabs and shrimps). In the neighborhood/landscape models, coral volume, neighborhood density, and site did not predict condition (all p > 0.30; Fig. S5).

## Supporting results (Supplement)

### Neighborhood effects

Neighborhood density (n_neighbors) did not significantly predict CAFI abundance (β = −0.005, z = −0.89, p = 0.37; Fig. S5; Table S5) or species richness (β = −0.003, z = −0.88, p = 0.38) after controlling for coral volume and site. However, mean neighbor distance showed a significant negative association with richness (β = −0.005, z = −3.20, p = 0.001) and Shannon diversity (β = −0.007, t = −3.49, p = 0.001), suggesting that corals closer to neighbors support slightly higher diversity, though this pattern was not observed for total abundance. No size × neighborhood interactions were significant (all p > 0.10). The power analysis (n = 61) indicated 65% power for medium effects (R² = 0.10), so null results for density may reflect insufficient power. Compositional variability differed among neighborhood density categories (PERMDISP: F = 29.9, p = 0.001), though the linear trend in distance-to-centroid was not significant (β = 5.3 × 10⁻⁴, p = 0.53).

### Co-occurrence patterns

Volume-weighted null models revealed no significant pairwise co-occurrence after FDR correction (0 of 528 pairs significant at FDR < 0.05; Fig. S9; Table S6), though two pairs approached significance: *Harpiliopsis beaupresii*–*H. spinigera* (SES = −3.42, p_FDR = 0.053) and *H. beaupresii*–*Paragobiodon modestus* (SES = −3.47, p_FDR = 0.053). Intraspecific density analysis identified six species with significant mating-pair aggregation (FDR < 0.05): *Synalpheus charon* (SES = 10.5), *Alpheus lottini* (SES = 10.1), *Harpiliopsis beaupresii* (SES = 5.4), *Trapezia serenei* (SES = 4.4), *Alpheus pachychirus* (SES = 4.0), and *Trapezia bidentata* (SES = 3.7; Table S7).

### Cross-study scaling concordance

Seven species had scaling data in both the survey and companion experiment. Of these, two showed sublinear scaling in the survey but proportional scaling during experimental colonization (*Calcinus latens*, *Alpheus lottini*), one was sublinear in both studies (*Trapezia serenei*), and four scaled proportionally in both (*Caracanthus maculatus*, *Harpiliopsis spinigera*, *Galeropsis monodonta*, *Periclimenes watamuae*; Table S8).
