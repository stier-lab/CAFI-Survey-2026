# Introduction

The species--area relationship is one of the oldest and most general patterns in ecology: larger habitats support more species (Rosenzweig 1995). Yet the mechanisms generating this pattern remain debated. In its simplest form, the relationship reflects passive sampling -- larger areas accumulate more individuals and therefore more species (Connor & McCoy 1979). But when habitats are discrete patches embedded in a landscape, active processes intervene. Propagule supply is finite, so adding habitat can redistribute settlers rather than generate new ones (Osenberg et al. 2002). Habitat size governs not only how many occupants arrive but which species establish, how they interact, and -- through biodiversity--ecosystem function (BEF) feedbacks -- how occupants in turn modify the habitat itself (Tilman et al. 2014). Understanding how these sequential links connect habitat size to occupant communities to habitat condition is a central challenge for community and landscape ecology. It also has direct implications for habitat conservation and restoration.

Branching corals offer a powerful model system for studying these general questions. Individual coral colonies function as discrete habitat islands for the crustaceans, fishes, gastropods, and other invertebrates that shelter within their branches -- collectively termed coral-associated fauna and invertebrates (CAFI; Abele & Patton 1976; Stella et al. 2011). Like forest patches or host plants, coral colonies vary in size over orders of magnitude, are embedded in landscapes of varying density and spacing, and support occupant communities whose diversity has measurable consequences for habitat condition. Three habitat dimensions independently influence CAFI communities: habitat amount controls regional propagule supply, habitat size sets local carrying capacity, and habitat proximity mediates inter-patch dispersal (Stier & Osenberg 2010). Here we focus on habitat size, the dimension that most directly links individual-level species--area scaling to community assembly and ecosystem function.

How should CAFI abundance scale with coral size? Two competing hypotheses frame the question. The Field of Dreams hypothesis predicts proportional scaling (abundance exponent β = 1): larger corals attract proportionally more settlers from a non-limiting propagule pool, so "if you build it, they will come" (Palmer et al. 1997). The Propagule Redirection hypothesis predicts sublinear scaling (β < 1): because the supply of colonists is finite, larger habitats dilute per-capita density as they draw settlers from the same pool (Osenberg et al. 2002; Stier & Osenberg 2010). Propagule Redirection predicts stronger sublinear scaling in established communities than in recently colonized ones, because density-dependent mortality, territorial exclusion, and priority effects intensify over time. We test this prediction by comparing our survey with a companion colonization experiment (Stier et al. in review).

The scaling of abundance with habitat size has direct consequences for community composition. If CAFI density dilutes on larger corals, then increasing total occupancy requires adding new species rather than more individuals of existing ones -- a process we term "species stacking," driven by the territorial behavior of obligate symbionts such as *Trapezia* guard crabs and *Alpheus* snapping shrimp, which occupy corals as single mating pairs and aggressively exclude conspecifics (Castro 1978; Huber & Coles 1986; Stier et al. 2012). Species stacking predicts that composition should turn over along the size gradient, with larger corals accumulating functionally distinct taxa rather than simply nested subsets of the small-coral fauna. Whether this turnover produces genuine compositional divergence after controlling for the abundance--richness correlation determines how sensitive community structure is to the loss of particular size classes.

Composition matters because CAFI are not passive occupants of coral habitat; they are active agents that modify it. Guard crabs defend their coral hosts against corallivorous sea stars and clear suffocating sediment (Glynn 1983; Stewart et al. 2006). Coral-dwelling fishes provide nutrient subsidies that accelerate coral growth by 50--100% (Holbrook et al. 2008) and buffer bleaching severity (Chase et al. 2018). Critically, these services combine synergistically when diverse mutualists cohabit a coral: crabs and shrimp together reduce tissue loss far beyond the sum of their individual effects (McKeon et al. 2012). But corals also host antagonists -- coralliophiline snails feed on coral tissue (Baums et al. 2003) and vermetid snails reduce coral growth (Shima et al. 2010). The net effect on coral condition therefore depends on the balance between mutualists and parasites, which is itself governed by how diversity scales with habitat size. This creates a hypothesized feedback loop: coral size structures CAFI density and diversity (Q1), which in turn shapes community composition and functional balance (Q2), and the resulting community may modify coral condition (Q3) -- potentially feeding back to coral size over time (Stier & Osenberg 2024b). CAFI are underappreciated drivers of coral resilience whose contribution scales with the diversity rules we seek to quantify.

This BEF framework -- in which habitat size shapes occupant diversity, and occupant diversity in turn influences habitat condition -- is well established in plant community experiments (Tilman et al. 2014; Cardinale et al. 2012) but remains largely untested for animal communities on biogenic habitat. Testing BEF in established communities matters because they differ from experimentally assembled ones in fundamental ways: priority effects filter colonizers through resident aggression (Counsell & Donahue 2021), density-dependent mortality erases variation in initial settlement (Schmitt et al. 2007), and territorial species monopolize space in ways that constrain community trajectories (Stier et al. 2012). These post-settlement processes may weaken BEF signals by homogenizing communities, or strengthen them by selecting for complementary species combinations.

Here we present a survey of CAFI communities across 114 *Pocillopora* colonies spanning three reef habitats in Mo'orea, French Polynesia. We catalogued 3,989 individuals from 243 taxa (154 identified to species, 89 to genus or higher) and address three questions that together trace the links in the habitat size--diversity--function feedback loop:

**Q1 (Scaling):** How do CAFI abundance, richness, and per-capita density scale with coral colony volume? We test the Field of Dreams (β = 1), Propagule Redirection (β < 1), and super-linear (β > 1) hypotheses using negative binomial GLMs with site-stratified bootstrap confidence intervals, and extend the analysis to 21 individual species and six taxonomic groups.

**Q2 (Composition):** Does coral size drive compositional turnover beyond passive sampling? We test whether site-level species pools and continuous colony size gradients independently structure community composition using PERMANOVA, distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA), and nestedness analysis, controlling for the abundance--richness confound through iterated rarefaction.

**Q3 (Feedbacks):** Does CAFI diversity predict coral physiological condition? Using a BEF framework, we test whether species richness and total abundance independently predict a multivariate index of coral condition, employing variance partitioning and path analysis to disentangle the diversity pathway from the abundance pathway.

These three questions form a hypothesized causal chain: Q1 establishes how habitat size shapes occupant quantity and diversity, Q2 tests whether this diversity translates into genuine compositional differentiation, and Q3 asks whether the resulting community feeds back to affect habitat condition. This study complements a companion experiment that manipulated *Pocillopora* density over 17 months (Stier et al. in review). Where the experiment captures colonization dynamics under Field of Dreams conditions, the survey provides ecological realism in established communities where Propagule Redirection and density dependence have had time to shape the assemblage.
