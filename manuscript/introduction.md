# Introduction

The relationship between habitat quantity and occupant abundance is among the most general patterns in ecology (Connor & McCoy 1979; Preston 1962), yet whether abundance scales proportionally with habitat or sublinearly remains unresolved. The answer controls per-unit occupant density and, where the habitat is itself a living organism, the strength of occupant--host feedbacks. In ecosystems structured by foundation species -- trees, corals, kelps, oysters -- the scaling regime therefore shapes both community structure and host condition (Dayton 1972; MacArthur & Wilson 1967). Proportional scaling maintains constant per-unit density; sublinear scaling dilutes occupants in larger patches and concentrates them in smaller ones.

The scaling regime matters most when the habitat is biogenic and responsive to occupant density. Residents of biogenic habitats alter host growth, survival, and condition through feeding (Silliman et al. 2005), nutrient processing (Meyer & Schultz 1985), predator defense (Glynn 1983), and interactions with co-residents (Angelini et al. 2011). Per-unit occupant density therefore governs the intensity of occupant--host interactions, linking habitat configuration to habitat condition. Theoretical models recognize this link (Hamman et al. 2018; Moeller et al. 2023), but empirical tests remain scarce.

Two competing hypotheses predict how occupant density scales with habitat quantity. Under the Field of Dreams hypothesis, colonists arrive in proportion to habitat, maintaining constant per-unit density -- "if you build it, they will come" (Palmer et al. 1997; Resetarits & Binckley 2009). Propagule Redirection posits the opposite: patches draw from a shared colonist pool, so adding habitat dilutes per-unit density (Carr & Hixon 1997; Stier & Osenberg 2010). Both regimes can operate simultaneously -- juveniles may redistribute among patches while adults track habitat proportionally -- and the outcome depends on colonist longevity, density-dependent mortality, and migration frequency (Hamman et al. 2018).

Each regime carries different consequences for the habitat itself. Under Propagule Redirection, small patches accumulate disproportionately high occupant densities, enhancing condition if occupants provide benefits but suppressing it if they impose costs (Bissett et al. 2025; Stier & Osenberg 2024a). Field of Dreams, by contrast, holds density invariant, decoupling habitat configuration from occupant-mediated effects. Which regime prevails thus shapes ecosystem trajectories: as habitat loss restructures occupant communities, it alters the feedbacks that influence recovery (Rennick et al. 2022).

Scaling regime and habitat size jointly determine which species occupy a given patch, so community variation along the size gradient should itself influence habitat condition. Biodiversity--ecosystem function (BEF) theory predicts that functionally diverse communities enhance ecosystem performance through complementarity (Loreau & Hector 2001). In habitat-occupant systems, residents defend hosts against consumers, recycle limiting nutrients, or consume host tissue; the net effect on condition depends on which functional roles are represented, not just total abundance. No study has tested the full chain -- from habitat quantity through community assembly to habitat condition -- in a biogenic habitat system.

Branching corals in the genus *Pocillopora* Lamarck, 1816 offer a tractable model. *Pocillopora* colonies create complex three-dimensional structures supporting dense assemblages of coral-associated fishes and invertebrates (CAFI), sometimes exceeding 65 individuals from over 20 species on a single colony (Curtis et al. 2023). Guard crabs (*Trapezia* Latreille, 1828 spp.) defend corals from predatory sea stars and remove sediment (Glynn 1983; Stier et al. 2012). Snapping shrimp (*Alpheus* Fabricius, 1798 spp.) also deter predators and competitors. Coral-dwelling fishes subsidize host nutrition through ammonia-rich excretion (Holbrook et al. 2008; Shantz et al. 2023). Corallivorous gastropods consume tissue and facilitate disease (Boucher 1986; Nicolet et al. 2013). These beneficial and harmful roles make CAFI diversity a candidate driver of coral condition under BEF theory -- but only if composition changes predictably with colony size.

Because many CAFI are obligate coral associates that occupy colonies as territorial mating pairs (Castro 1978; Huber & Coles 1986), intraspecific density on a single colony is effectively capped. As total occupancy rises on larger corals, growth comes predominantly from adding species rather than more conspecifics -- "species stacking," whereby intraspecific density ceilings force diversification (Stier et al. 2012). If species stacking operates, it generates a compositional prediction distinct from passive sampling: small-coral faunas should not be nested subsets of large-coral faunas, and colony volume should structure the identity of occupants, not just their number.

CAFI abundance increases with colony volume but less than proportionally (Gotelli & Abele 1985; Curtis et al. 2023), a pattern consistent across coral genera and reef systems (Harborne et al. 2011; Brush et al. 2026). Scaling slopes vary widely among taxa -- from 0.06 to 0.64 across decapod species (Gotelli & Abele 1985) -- and colony-scale variation can explain up to 84% of cryptofaunal richness variance (Counsell et al. 2018). A companion experiment manipulating coral density (1, 3, or 6 colonies per 25 m^2^) found proportional scaling during active colonization (Stier et al. in review), suggesting that post-settlement processes may shift the scaling regime.

Three links in this hypothesized chain remain untested in established communities: whether abundance and richness scale sublinearly with colony volume, whether composition turns over or merely nests along the size gradient, and whether the resulting community variation covaries with coral condition independently of abundance. Here, we survey 114 *Pocillopora* colonies across three reef habitats on Mo'orea, French Polynesia, cataloging 3,989 individuals from 243 taxa to address three questions:

**Q1 (Scaling):** Does CAFI abundance scale sublinearly with coral colony volume, consistent with Propagule Redirection? We predicted sublinear scaling and density dilution. We further predicted that the raw species--area relationship (SAR) would reflect passive sampling -- the "more individuals" hypothesis (Srivastava & Lawton 1998) -- rather than active species accumulation. As a corollary, we tested whether proximity to neighboring colonies augments CAFI diversity beyond what focal volume alone explains (n = 61 colonies with neighborhood surveys).

If abundance scales sublinearly, species stacking predicts that composition should turn over -- not merely accumulate -- along the size gradient.

**Q2 (Composition):** Does CAFI composition change along the coral size gradient through species turnover or nestedness? We predicted turnover rather than nestedness, with colony volume structuring occupant identity beyond abundance alone.

**Q3 (Condition):** Does CAFI species richness predict coral physiological condition independently of abundance? We predicted that complementarity among functionally distinct residents -- guard crabs, nutrient-subsidizing fishes, corallivorous gastropods -- would generate a diversity--condition relationship beyond the richness--abundance correlation.

Together, these questions trace a hypothesized chain of associations from habitat quantity through community assembly to habitat condition, extending scaling patterns observed during experimental colonization (Stier et al. in review) to established communities on a natural size gradient. If occupant density and composition depend predictably on habitat size, then habitat loss and restoration restructure the communities that feed back on habitat condition -- a prediction made more urgent by the size-dependent vulnerability of large habitat-forming organisms to climate stress, and as relevant to oyster reefs and forest canopies as to coral branches.
