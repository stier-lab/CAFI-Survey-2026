## INTRODUCTION THEMES

### 1. Habitat Size → Fauna Abundance & Richness (Scaling Relationships)

Habitat size, primarily measured as coral colony volume or surface area, is a fundamental driver of community structure, abundance, and species richness for marine cryptofauna and associated fishes. Across the literature, larger coral colonies consistently harbor greater numbers of species, individuals, and total biomass, though these relationships are strongly mediated by the biological and territorial traits of the resident species.

**Key Species-Area and Abundance-Area Scaling Relationships**
- **Logarithmic Species-Area Scaling:** Abele & Patton (1976) demonstrated a highly significant logarithmic species-area relationship for decapod crustaceans on *Pocillopora damicornis* (log species = 0.356 log area - 0.316). Coral area accounted for nearly six-fold differences in species numbers (4-22 species).
- **The Species Asymptote:** The cumulative species-area curve becomes asymptotic at ~6,000 cm³. Beyond this size, no entirely new species are added; larger coral heads allow co-occurrence of species that would normally inhabit separate small corals.
- **Abundance and Inverse Density Scaling:** Total population sizes of decapods scale with coral area (log individuals = 0.618 log area - 0.841). Because slope < 1, **density of individuals per unit area decreases on larger corals**.
- **Biomass Scaling:** Barry (1965) found a strong linear correlation between total crustacean biomass and estimated surface area of the host coral.
- **Advanced Metrology:** Curtis et al. (2023) showed that 3D photogrammetry-based estimates of colony skeletal volume are the most accurate predictors of coral-associated fish and invertebrate abundance and richness.

**Mechanisms Structuring the Community**
- **Extinction and Population Dynamics:** Small coral heads support small populations with higher local extinction probability and high turnover (Abele & Patton 1976).
- **Recruitment Constraints:** Coral volume is the best predictor of *Trapezia* recruitment (Canizales-Flores et al. 2021). Damselfish group size scales with colony size and branch density (Chase et al. 2014).
- **Territoriality and Social Limits:** Mobile species increase rapidly with volume; territorial species (like *Trapezia*) do not (Julian Caley et al. 2001; Huber & Coles 1986). *Trapezia* occupy as a single pair and exclude conspecifics. Increasing total symbiont abundance requires increasing species diversity (Stier et al. 2012).
- **Scale of Impact:** Colony-scale variation accounts for ~85% of variance in species richness (Counsell et al. 2018). If local host size range is narrow, total habitat availability overrides colony-level effects (Pisapia et al. 2020).

**Key citations:** Abele & Patton 1976; Barry 1965; Curtis et al. 2023; Canizales-Flores et al. 2021; Chase et al. 2014; Julian Caley et al. 2001; Huber & Coles 1986; Stier et al. 2012; Counsell et al. 2018; Pisapia et al. 2020

### 2. CAFI Mutualism & Biodiversity-Ecosystem Function (BEF)

The mutualistic relationships between hard corals and their associated fauna are foundational to the health, growth, and resilience of coral reef ecosystems.

**Invertebrate Mutualists: Crabs and Shrimp**
- **Defense Against Corallivores:** Glynn (1976, 1980, 1983) established that *Trapezia* crabs and *Alpheus* shrimp aggressively defend host corals against *Acanthaster* and *Culcita*. Rouze et al. (2014): juvenile *Trapezia* protect juvenile corals, increasing survival by 32%.
- **Sediment Clearance (Housekeeping):** Stewart et al. (2006, 2013): guard crabs sweep sediment. Corals lacking crabs suffered 45-80% mortality from sediment smothering within a month; all corals with crabs survived with faster growth and less bleaching.
- **Parasite Mitigation:** Stier et al. (2010): *Trapezia serenei* alleviates growth-stunting effects of parasitic vermetid snails (*Dendropoma maximum*).

**Fish Mutualists**
- **Nutrient Provisioning:** Meyer et al. (1983) and Holbrook et al. (2008): fish excrete ammonium and phosphorus, enhancing *Symbiodinium* density and coral skeletal growth (50-100% higher).
- **Water Aeration:** Goldshmid et al. (2004) and Garcia-Herrera et al. (2017): damselfish "sleep-swimming" prevents hypoxia, boosting photosynthetic rates by 3-6%.
- **Algae Removal:** Dixson & Hay (2012): *Acropora nasuta* releases odor when contacted by toxic seaweed; gobies (*Gobiodon histrio*) trim and consume the algae.
- **Buffering Climate Stress:** Chase et al. (2018): corals hosting *Dascyllus aruanus* maintained higher *Symbiodinium* densities during thermal stress.

**BEF Literature**
- **Multiple Defender Effects (Synergy):** McKeon et al. (2012): crab + shrimp combined reduced coral tissue consumed by 73% (emergent synergy).
- **Diversity Overcomes Density Limits:** Stier et al. (2012): maximizing sediment removal (48% clearance) requires diverse multi-species assemblage to bypass intraspecific competition limits.
- **Size-Class Complementarity:** McKeon & Moore (2014): different sizes/species of *Trapezia* defend against different predators. Small crabs for *Drupella*, large crabs for *Acanthaster*.

**Key citations:** Glynn 1976, 1980, 1983; Rouze et al. 2014; Stewart et al. 2006, 2013; Stier et al. 2010, 2012; Holbrook et al. 2008; Goldshmid et al. 2004; Garcia-Herrera et al. 2017; Dixson & Hay 2012; Chase et al. 2018; McKeon et al. 2012; McKeon & Moore 2014

### 3. Drivers of CAFI Community Composition

**Host Identity and Coral Health**
- Stella et al. (2010): 15% of 178 taxa exclusive to a single host species on GBR; pocilloporids had higher epifaunal abundance than acroporids.
- Pratchett (2001): clear distinction between pocilloporid vs acroporid symbionts (*Tetralia* + *Gobiodon* in acroporids; *Trapezia* + *Alpheus* in pocilloporids).
- Dead coral transitions community from obligate specialists to facultative non-obligate species (Leray et al. 2012). Stella et al. (2022): rubble and dead coral hosted greatest density/diversity of motile cryptofauna.

**Coral Morphology (Size and Complexity)**
- Species-area relationship becomes asymptotic at ~6000 cm³ (Abele & Patton 1976).
- Tightly branched corals provide better refuge and host higher abundances.
- *Trapezia intermedia* shows unique preference for smaller colonies (Counsell et al. 2018).

**Environmental Gradients and Site Effects**
- Colony-scale variation = 84.2% of variance; site-scale = 5.2% (Counsell et al. 2018, Hawaii).
- Abundance/richness increases with wave height, decreases with chl-a.
- Depth partitions species: *T. digitalis* and *U. dimorpha* prefer shallow; *T. tigrina* prefers deep.
- Pisapia et al. (2020, Moorea): current flow and wave exposure did not significantly correlate with epifaunal assemblages.

**Co-Occurrence Patterns**
- *Trapezia* and *Alpheus* occur as single male-female pairs, excluding conspecifics (Stier et al. 2012).
- *T. serenei* and *A. lottini* co-occur more than expected by chance.
- Established mutualists act as gatekeepers limiting colonization of non-obligate decapods (Counsell & Donahue 2021).
- Congeneric species rarely co-occur on same coral (Abele & Patton 1976).
- Predators (*Neocirrhites*, *Caracanthus*) reduce cryptofauna abundance by 34% and richness by 20% (Stier & Leray 2014).

**Key citations:** Stella et al. 2010, 2022; Pratchett 2001; Leray et al. 2012; Counsell et al. 2018; Pisapia et al. 2020; Stier et al. 2012; Counsell & Donahue 2021; Stier & Leray 2014

### 4. Propagule Redirection vs. Field of Dreams

**Propagule Redirection** (Osenberg et al. 2002): Adding new habitat patches draws arriving colonists away from existing sites. Total settlers in the landscape remain constant; density per patch decreases. Benefits overall population by reducing density-dependent mortality.

**Field of Dreams** (Palmer et al. 1997): Increasing habitat leads to proportionate increase in total new colonists. New habitat captures organisms that would not have otherwise settled; no decrease in density at existing sites.

**Core Contrast:** Field of Dreams assumes colonists are not limiting and new habitat generates *new* local production. Propagule Redirection assumes limited colonist supply; new habitat redistributes the existing pool, creating "settlement shadows."

**Empirical Evidence**
- **Propagule Redirection:** Stier & Osenberg (2010, Moorea): isolated focal reefs received 2-4x more larval fish than focal reefs surrounded by neighbors. Results fell midway: redirection occurred, but total colonization still increased 1.3-fold for 6-fold area increase. Modeling projected habitat addition benefits fish by ameliorating density-dependent competition.
- **Field of Dreams:** Resetarits & Binckley (2009, aquatic beetles): per-pond colonization rate independent of pond density; total colonization increased proportionately with pools.

**Key citations:** Osenberg et al. 2002; Palmer et al. 1997; Stier & Osenberg 2010; Resetarits & Binckley 2009; Hamman 2017

### 5. Sublinear Scaling & Density Dilution Mechanisms

**Studies Documenting Sublinear Scaling**
- Abele & Patton (1976): slope = 0.618 (individuals vs area); density inversely correlated with coral size.
- Gotelli et al. (1985): slopes ranged 0.06-0.64 across 7 decapod species, all < 1.
- Hamman (2017): theoretical models confirm settler density decreases as colony grows.

**Mechanisms**
- **Territorial Expansion by Mating Pairs:** Key obligate symbionts (*A. lottini*, *T. ferruginea*) reside as single pair regardless of coral size. As coral grows, pairs expand territories and exclude conspecifics → density drops.
- **Space Requirements and Exclusion:** Aggressive dominants monopolize small corals. On larger corals, once minimum territory is claimed, "excess" habitat must be colonized by other species.
- **Scaling of Settlement Signals vs Area:** If attraction signals scale with radius (perimeter) rather than area, settler rate grows slower than area → dilution (Hamman 2017).

**Key citations:** Abele & Patton 1976; Gotelli et al. 1985; Hamman 2017; Barry 1965

### 6. Observational vs. Experimental: Colonization vs. Mature Communities

**Observational surveys** reveal communities structured by strict territoriality, competitive exclusion, and host morphology. Mature corals host very few individuals per species (typically mating pairs). Congeneric species rarely co-occur.

**Experimental manipulations** reveal intense biotic pressures during colonization:
- Stier et al. (2014): predatory fishes substantially reduce colonizing cryptofauna abundance and diversity.
- Stier & Osenberg (2010): isolated patches receive more settlers (propagule redirection).

**Post-settlement filters explaining the transition:**
- **Priority effects:** First colonizers dictate trajectory. Established *Alpheus* inhibit conspecific colonization; mutualists account for 39% of early composition variation, diluting to 20% after 6 months as communities converge (Counsell et al. 2021).
- **Density-dependent mortality:** Large initial cohorts rapidly reduced by competition for enemy-free space. High-abundance settlement → low-density mating pairs in mature state.
- **Host ontogeny:** Mature corals provide more space but resident mutualists defend regardless of size → one pair per species whether small or large.

**Key citations:** Stier et al. 2012, 2014; Stier & Osenberg 2010; Counsell et al. 2021; Hamman 2017; Head et al. 2015

### 7. Corallivory & Effects on Coral Condition

**Gastropods**
- *Coralliophila* (e.g., *C. violacea*, *C. abbreviata*): "prudent sessile feeders" that insert proboscis into polyps. Create localized energy sinks; depress growth rates; can vector white band disease.
- *Drupella* (e.g., *D. cornus*, *D. rugosa*): scrape tissue with radula, leaving white feeding scars. Form massive aggregations; vector brown band disease.

**Fish Corallivores**
- Excavating bites costly to heal (tissue + skeleton regeneration). Reduce growth and reproduction.
- *Pocillopora* shows "reoriented growth" under pufferfish grazing: shorter/thicker to maintain carbonate production (Palacios et al. 2014).
- Fish corallivory can facilitate turf algae and pathogenic bacteria; some butterflyfishes slow disease progression by consuming infected tissue.

**Mediating Factors**
- **Mutualistic Defenders:** *Trapezia*, *Alpheus* actively repel *Acanthaster*, *Culcita*, *Drupella*.
- **Coral Condition:** Snails attracted to damaged/stressed/diseased corals → feedback loop of intensified predation.
- **Temperature & Bleaching:** Chronic corallivory drains energy reserves, reducing bleaching resilience.
- **Nutrient Pollution:** Complex context-dependent effects. Excess nutrients exacerbate mortality in excavating predation but can sometimes mitigate snail-caused tissue mortality.
- **Overfishing:** Releases invertebrate corallivores from predation pressure → outbreaks.

### 8. Conservation Implications, Feedbacks & Future Directions

**Mutualist Services for Resilience**
- Resident fishes and decapods buffer corals against stressors. Diverse mutualist communities provide superior resilience via complementarity ("species stacking," multiple defender effects).
- Coral increasingly viewed as "metaorganism" (host + algae + microbes + exosymbionts).

**Disruption and Feedback Loops**
- **Coral Size Loss:** Larger colonies host more diverse mutualists and drive most reproductive output. Marine heatwaves disproportionately kill large colonies → cascading cryptofauna declines → reduced recovery capacity (Speare et al. 2022).
- **Bleaching:** Reduces coral nutritional quality (lipid-rich mucus) → 85% decline in obligate crab egg production, reduced defense, forced emigration (Stella 2011). Extreme heat flips mutualism to antagonism: dominant males evict females and shrimp (Stella et al. 2014).
- **Habitat Degradation:** Obligate mutualists replaced by facultative non-obligate community. Dead substrate promotes macroalgae → suppresses coral recruitment → locks reef into degraded state.
- **Positive → Negative Feedback:** Healthy: growing coral → more refuge → more mutualists → services enhance growth. Disturbed: stress → fewer/weaker mutualists → coral more vulnerable to predation/sediment/disease → further decline.

**Future Directions**
- Scale individual-colony mutualism benefits to population dynamics and seascape-level recovery.
- Build a "service catalog" matching mutualists to coral taxa for restoration co-transplantation.
- Multi-stressor interaction thresholds (warming × nutrients × corallivory).
- Metagenomic approaches: how macro-symbionts influence coral microbiome and disease resistance.

**Key citations:** Speare et al. 2022; Stella et al. 2011, 2014, 2022; Leray et al. 2012; Chase et al. 2018; Stier et al. 2024; Adam et al. 2022; Fong et al. 2023; Rice et al. 2019
