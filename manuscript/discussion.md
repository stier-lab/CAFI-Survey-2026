# Discussion

Our survey of 114 *Pocillopora* colonies across three reef habitats in Mo'orea reveals that coral size predicts the quantity, identity, and functional consequences of associated fauna in established reef communities. CAFI abundance and richness scale sublinearly with coral volume, consistent with Propagule Redirection and density dilution (Fig. 2). Community composition is structured by both reef-scale species pools and a continuous coral size gradient that drives species turnover rather than passive accumulation (Fig. 4). And CAFI species richness positively predicts coral physiological condition, with variance partitioning suggesting a role for diversity beyond abundance alone (Fig. 5). Together, these three results are consistent with a feedback loop in which coral size structures CAFI communities, whose diversity in turn may feed back on coral condition. Because the survey is cross-sectional and observational, we can demonstrate associations consistent with each link in this hypothesized feedback but cannot establish causality; we interpret our findings in light of the experimental literature throughout.

## Sublinear scaling and the mechanistic basis for Propagule Redirection

Sublinear scaling of occupant diversity with habitat size is widespread: epiphyte communities on tropical trees show decelerating species accumulation with trunk diameter (Zotz & Vollrath 2003), kelp holdfast invertebrates scale sublinearly with holdfast volume (Anderson et al. 2005), and cavity-nesting bird communities in old-growth forests depend on the largest trees for diverse nesting sites (van der Hoek et al. 2017). In each case, a central question is whether larger habitats attract proportionally more colonists or whether a finite propagule supply imposes density dilution.

Our data resolve this question for coral-associated fauna. The scaling exponent for total CAFI abundance (β = 0.52 [0.44, 0.62]) falls well below proportional scaling and is comparable to Abele and Patton's (1976) foundational estimate of 0.62 from *Pocillopora damicornis* in the Gulf of Panama (noting that their estimate used colony area rather than volume as the size metric). The pattern was consistent across species and groups: the inverse-variance-weighted mean across 21 species was β = 0.51 [0.46, 0.56], five of six taxonomic groups scaled sublinearly, and results were robust across five taxonomic resolution scenarios (Fig. S6). The sole exception was gastropods (β = 0.94), a group dominated by the coralliophiline snail *Galeropsis monodonta* (73% of all gastropods). The near-proportional scaling likely reflects this single species' relationship with coral volume rather than a group-level property.

The mechanistic basis for Propagule Redirection is well established. Stier and Osenberg (2010) used a Beverton--Holt model to decompose recruitment gains when habitat is added: 114% of the recruitment increase came from reduced density dependence (Propagule Redirection) while only 16% came from genuinely new settlement (Field of Dreams). Chemical settlement cues scale with colony radius rather than volume, creating "signal halos" whose effective interception area grows more slowly than habitat capacity (Hamman et al. 2018).

Obligate symbionts compound this through territorial exclusion: *Trapezia* crabs and *Alpheus* shrimp occupy corals as single mating pairs regardless of colony size (Huber & Coles 1986; Castro 1978), so per-capita density necessarily declines as volume increases. Increasing total CAFI on a colony requires adding new species, not more individuals -- the constraint Stier et al. (2012) identified as linking diversity to ecosystem function.

The relative importance of Field of Dreams versus Propagule Redirection depends on ambient settlement intensity (Stier & Osenberg 2010). At low settlement, the propagule pool is exhausted quickly and Propagule Redirection dominates; at high settlement, the system saturates and Field of Dreams takes over. Our strong sublinear scaling suggests Mo'orea operates in the PR-dominated regime, but this settlement-intensity dependence may explain geographic variation in scaling exponents and offers a testable prediction: reefs with higher larval supply should show exponents closer to 1.0.

The rarefied richness analysis reveals that when richness is standardized to a common abundance (n = 20 individuals), the scaling relationship disappears (slope = 0.14, p = 0.64) -- the raw species--area relationship is largely an abundance artifact. Nevertheless, in natural communities, larger colonies do host more species, and this diversity is positively associated with coral condition regardless of whether it arises through active selection or passive accumulation.

### From colonization to establishment: a temporal shift in scaling regime

The transition from proportional to sublinear scaling as communities mature parallels general colonization dynamics: recently disturbed islands show rapid species accumulation that decelerates toward equilibrium as density-dependent interactions intensify (MacArthur & Wilson 1967). The companion experimental study (Stier et al. in review) found proportional scaling during active colonization, while our survey of established communities reveals sublinear scaling.

The Beverton--Holt framework explains this transition quantitatively. During colonization, settlers arrive roughly proportionally to available habitat. But post-settlement density dependence -- territorial exclusion, priority effects, predation -- progressively reduces per-capita survival. Even when new settlement accounted for only 16% of recruitment gains, total recruitment to newly added habitat increased substantially because Propagule Redirection alleviated density-dependent mortality on existing patches (Stier & Osenberg 2010). Over time, as mating pairs establish and exclude conspecifics, the community converges on the sublinear equilibrium.

Species-level data support this interpretation: *Alpheus lottini* and *Calcinus latens* scaled proportionally during colonization but sublinearly in established communities, while *Trapezia serenei* was sublinear in both contexts -- consistent with early territorial density ceilings. However, with only 2 of 7 overlapping species showing the predicted shift, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously.

This temporal dimension has practical implications: initial recruitment surges following restoration will attenuate as communities mature, and planning that assumes proportional returns will overestimate long-term gains.

## Community composition: site pools, size gradients, and species turnover

Community composition across habitat patches reflects regional species pools filtered by local habitat characteristics -- a pattern documented from forest fragments (Laurance et al. 2002) to intertidal rock pools (Metaxas & Scheibling 1993). Our analyses reveal two independent compositional axes: reef site and coral volume, each significant after controlling for the other (Fig. 4). Sites exhibited distinct taxonomic signatures -- Maatea dominated by hermit crabs, Maharepa by obligate symbionts, Hauru by the most balanced community -- robust across five distance metrics and 500 subsampling iterations.

The continuous size gradient (db-RDA: 7.8%, F = 9.74, p = 0.001; rarefied: 2.6%, p = 0.001) confirmed genuine compositional turnover rather than an abundance artifact. Critically, this turnover was not nested (NODF = 18.4, z = −1.09, p = 0.28): small-coral faunas are not subsets of large-coral faunas. Some species were gained (*Trapezia punctimanus* was most strongly associated with large corals) while others were lost. This parallels epiphyte communities on tropical trees, where species replacement rather than nested accumulation drives compositional change along the trunk-diameter gradient (Burns & Dawson 2005). A similar pattern occurs in ant-myrmecophyte systems, where competitively dominant species displace early colonizers as hosts grow (Fonseca & Ganade 1996).

Volume-weighted null models revealed no significant pairwise co-occurrence (0 of 528 pairs after FDR correction; Fig. S9), contrasting with strong intraspecific mating-pair aggregation in six species -- consistent with the obligate-pair structure of *Trapezia* and *Alpheus* (Castro 1978; Stier et al. 2012). The near-absence of interspecific co-occurrence, after controlling for volume-driven occurrence probabilities, suggests habitat filtering dominates over interspecific interactions in structuring these communities.

## CAFI diversity and coral condition: evidence for a BEF relationship

The hypothesis that biodiversity enhances ecosystem function is among ecology's most influential ideas (Tilman et al. 2014), yet BEF research has focused overwhelmingly on plant communities (Cardinale et al. 2012). Ant-plant mutualisms provide the closest analogue to our system: diverse ant assemblages on myrmecophytic trees deliver more complete herbivore defense (Palmer et al. 2010), just as diverse crustacean assemblages provide complementary defense for corals (McKeon et al. 2012).

Species richness significantly predicted coral physiological condition (Hochberg p = 0.036; Fig. 5A), and the direction and magnitude of this effect were robust to the choice of inference method (OLS primary, HC3 sensitivity). The critical question is whether this relationship reflects genuine BEF complementarity or merely an abundance confound, given the strong correlation between richness and abundance (r = 0.77).

Several observations favor the complementarity interpretation, though none is individually decisive. Variance partitioning attributed 29.1% of explained variance uniquely to richness but less than 1% uniquely to abundance, with 70.8% shared (Fig. S10A). The path model showed a directionally consistent pattern, with the standardized coefficient from richness to condition (β = 0.55) larger in magnitude than from abundance to condition (β = 0.02), though neither individual path reached significance (p = 0.20 and p > 0.50, respectively; Fig. S10C). Additionally, no individual species survived multiple-testing correction -- a pattern consistent with, but not diagnostic of, complementarity-driven BEF in plant systems (Hector et al. 1999), though it is also consistent with insufficient power to detect individual species effects given the sample size (n = 84) and number of tests (95 species × trait combinations). The overall effect size was modest (adjusted R² = 0.04 for the richness model), consistent with a real but small diversity signal embedded in substantial unexplained variation.

The rarefied richness test (p = 0.50; Fig. S8; Fig. S11B) warrants careful interpretation. In Q1 (scaling), we used this test to conclude that the raw species--area relationship is largely an abundance artifact. Here in Q3, the same test could indicate either that the richness--condition relationship is also an abundance artifact, or that rarefaction removes the BEF mechanism itself (more species → fuller habitat use → more CAFI → better condition). These two contexts differ: in Q1, the question is whether species accumulate non-randomly with size, while in Q3, the causal pathway may run through abundance. We cannot resolve this ambiguity with the available data; the variance partitioning and path model provide more informative -- if imperfect -- evidence for separating diversity from abundance effects.

The experimental literature provides a strong mechanistic basis for the forward pathway, including defense against corallivores (Glynn 1983; Stier et al. 2012), nutrient subsidies (Holbrook et al. 2008), bleaching buffering (Chase et al. 2018), and synergistic defense (McKeon et al. 2012; McKeon & Moore 2014). Beyond the obligate symbionts described in the Introduction, an expanding catalog of facultative mutualists -- spider crabs removing macroalgae (Spadaro & Butler 2021), sea cucumbers consuming pathogenic bacteria (Clements et al. 2024), farmerfish buffering heat waves (Honeycutt et al. 2023) -- is consistent with the broad richness signal we detect rather than effects concentrated in a few taxa.

A key limitation is the observational nature of the BEF analysis. The positive richness--condition relationship could reflect reverse causation (healthier corals attract more species) or unmeasured confounding. The reverse direction (condition predicting CAFI richness) was non-significant in our data, though this does not rule out reverse causation. Coral volume was controlled in all models, and the experimental literature provides a strong mechanistic basis for the forward pathway, but causal inference awaits manipulative BEF experiments.

## Mutualism breakdown under stress and the *Galeropsis* puzzle

Mutualisms are context-dependent, shifting along a continuum from beneficial to antagonistic (Bronstein 1994). In ant-acacia systems, drought destabilizes the mutualism as ants tend scale insects that damage the host (Palmer et al. 2008). In corals, the cost-benefit balance normally favors the host: trapeziid crabs consume small amounts of tissue but preferentially target damaged or dying areas, potentially removing infection sites (Stier & Osenberg 2024a). Under warming, however, *Trapezia* abundance and egg production decline, and dominant males may evict females and shrimp to monopolize dwindling resources (Stella et al. 2014), converting cooperation into antagonism.

Negative CAFI also exist constitutively: vermetid snails smother tissue with mucus nets, cause colony flattening, and alter bacterial microbiomes (Shima et al. 2010; Zill et al. 2017). Coralliophiline gastropods, including *Galeropsis monodonta*, feed on coral tissue and can reduce the host's ability to withstand heatwaves (Stier & Osenberg 2024a). *Galeropsis* showed a positive (though non-significant) association with condition -- the opposite of what a corallivory effect would predict. One possibility is that healthier corals sustain larger corallivore populations without measurable decline; another is that tissue consumption is too low under ambient conditions to affect whole-colony physiology; a third is simply that the non-significant association reflects sampling variability. We cannot distinguish these explanations with our cross-sectional data, as the reverse direction (condition → CAFI) was also non-significant. If warming shifts the cost-benefit balance, the benign relationships we document under ambient conditions could become antagonistic -- a prediction testable through longitudinal monitoring of CAFI--condition relationships across thermal stress events.

## Closing the feedback loop: coral size, CAFI diversity, and reef resilience

The three research questions are not independent findings but components of a hypothesized bidirectional feedback loop. Coral size predicts CAFI density and diversity through sublinear scaling (Q1). Size-dependent composition shifts involve species turnover rather than nested accumulation (Q2). The resulting community may feed back on coral condition through complementary services (Q3), which in turn influences coral growth, survival, and future size.

The hypothesized size--diversity--condition feedback has three properties with conservation implications. First, sublinear scaling means the feedback weakens as corals grow -- a doubling of volume increases CAFI abundance by only 43% (since 2^0.52 = 1.43) -- imposing diminishing marginal returns that may stabilize the mutualism under ambient conditions. Second, species turnover means the functional composition of the feedback changes along the size gradient: small and large corals receive different services from partially non-overlapping species pools. Third, the variance partitioning suggests the association with condition operates more through diversity than abundance, though the overall effect is modest (adjusted R² = 0.04): losing species may degrade function even if total abundance is maintained.

The feedback loop we describe -- where habitat size predicts occupant diversity, which may feed back on habitat condition -- has parallels in ant-acacia, cavity-nesting bird, kelp holdfast, and seagrass mesograzer systems (Palmer et al. 2010; van der Hoek et al. 2017; Anderson et al. 2005; Orth et al. 1984). The generality of this pattern suggests that sublinear scaling and BEF feedbacks in habitat--occupant systems may reflect analogous processes -- finite propagule supply, territorial exclusion, service complementarity -- though whether these represent common principles or convergent outcomes remains untested.

If the feedback loop operates as hypothesized, it has implications for the conservation value of large corals. When marine heatwaves selectively kill large colonies (Speare et al. 2022), they would eliminate the CAFI communities that may buffer coral condition. Surviving smaller colonies have lower CAFI diversity (Q1), different composition (Q2), and potentially weaker mutualistic buffering (Q3). The non-nested composition suggests that species associated with large corals may not readily redistribute to smaller ones, though this inference rests on a non-significant nestedness test (NODF = 18.4, p = 0.28). The possibility of a negative spiral -- stressed corals losing mutualist defenders, becoming more vulnerable (Stella et al. 2011, 2014) -- parallels the cascading consequences of losing large old trees from forests (Lindenmayer et al. 2012), but testing this requires longitudinal data through disturbance events.

## Limitations and future directions

Several caveats temper our conclusions. The survey is a single temporal snapshot from three sites; seasonal and post-disturbance variation may alter scaling and BEF patterns. Colony selection was haphazard (non-random), which may introduce unknown biases. The BEF analysis (n = 84) has modest overall explanatory power (adjusted R² = 0.04) and limited power for individual species effects. The path model coefficients, while directionally informative, did not reach significance individually. Coral volume was approximated as an ellipsoid; 3D photogrammetry better captures structural complexity (Curtis et al. 2023). Neighborhood effects (n = 61) were underpowered for small effects, though the significant mean-neighbor-distance result hints at proximity effects on diversity that warrant further investigation. Spatial autocorrelation was assessed on raw variables rather than model residuals, which would provide a more definitive test of spatial non-independence.

Three priorities emerge. First, longitudinal tracking through bleaching events would test whether the diversity--condition feedback persists under thermal stress or breaks down -- and whether recovery follows the colonization-to-establishment trajectory. Second, manipulative BEF experiments with controlled CAFI diversity could test causality directly, building the "service catalog" Stier and Osenberg (2024b) advocate for restoration co-transplantation. Third, scaling analyses using photogrammetry across reef systems would test whether the exponent is a conserved community property or varies with host morphology, biogeography, and ambient settlement intensity -- the key moderator predicted by the Beverton--Holt framework (Stier & Osenberg 2010).

## Conclusion

Because scaling shifts from proportional during colonization to sublinear in established communities -- mediated by territorial exclusion and finite propagule supply -- the consequences of habitat loss depend not only on how much coral is removed but on how long the remaining community has had to mature. The sublinear exponent (β = 0.52) means the feedback weakens with size, stabilizing the mutualism under ambient conditions but rendering it vulnerable when large corals are lost. As reefs face accelerating size-selective mortality, understanding these scaling rules and feedback dynamics is essential for predicting how large-coral loss will cascade through the cryptofauna that may help maintain them.
